Vanitas Veritas · Katherine Titterton The Statement
vanitas and veritas are one swap of letters apart. the whole difference is a choice.

A big print pulls you across the room. A figure bowed over a sink, face blown to white and stippled into halftone, bringing up one gold blob. Vomit, or a nugget. You genuinely can’t tell. You reach the basin already in her posture — hunched, mid-purge, washing up or throwing up. The tap gives no water: a font that cannot clean you, nothing here to wash in but more image.

Vanitas is a 400-year-old genre — the Dutch still lifes crammed with skulls, dead flowers and soap bubbles, all murmuring the same thing. You are going to die; put down the goblet. The bubble was the favorite symbol. Homo bulla, man is a bubble: shiny, then gone. Mine are AI bubbles, twice over: the soap bubble the Dutch painted for fragility, and the one many of us fear we are standing inside.

The print is my self-portrait, hung as a mirror: your face lands on mine and wears the dissolve. I am not above the slop. Hold the eye on the glass and a small camera lends your face to the next dream, once; the basin dreams you, then lets you go down the drain. The photo is never kept.

The slop here is the pretty kind, over-rendered until it squeaks, and the piece answers it with the one thing a model cannot fake: a reflex. What the body brings up reads as waste and treasure at once — the joke, and the point. The gold is a borrowed colour, near enough the orange of the labs that build these models. The tool has signed its own confession.

The water is a GPU dreaming, every frame made as you watch, all of it running to the drain. Turn the handle off: no water, no dream. Reach in and it slips through your fingers. I freedive; you cannot hold water. Pilate washed his hands of the truth. This asks the reverse. The veritas is the hand that reaches anyway, and the taste that steers the machine: every dream leans toward eight pictures I chose. In a room of generated everything, that choice is the only ground truth left.

What it was saying

The Dutch Republic in the 1600s had just gotten rich, fast: global trade, a new merchant class, tulip futures, more imported luxury than anyone knew what to do with — and a rigid Calvinism that held your possessions cannot save you. Vanitas is that contradiction in paint.

A patron commissions his shells, gold and books, has them rendered gorgeously, then hangs a skull over the pile so the indulgence reads as a sermon. Have the luxury, moralise the luxury.

My swaps

Slop is our tulip mania — infinite cheap abundance, generated faster than anyone can value it. Vanitas told the 17th century its wealth was hollow while cashing the cheque; this piece says it to the feed.

Vanitas Veritas · Katherine Titterton The Object
a sink with no water,
so you can’t wash your hands of it.

The print rises from the sink on one post: freestanding, no wall. The surface is reflective and lit on purpose, so your own face lands on the bowed figure. A small camera hides behind it.

The screen in the bowl is the water, running to the drain. You reach for the tap the way you would to wash, and what comes is a dream.

It boots straight into the water; a power cut is just the tap turned off. The same water runs on the web as veritas, and a touch in either moves both. A QR code on the sink opens it: sink == sync, one water.
